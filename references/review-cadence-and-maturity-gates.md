# Review Cadence and Maturity Gates

## Review cadence

A cadence only earns its place on the calendar if each meeting has a specific job. Match the frequency to the decision that meeting is actually supposed to make.

**Weekly.** A short check on the deal registration pipeline (which registrations are new, which are stalling, which are about to expire) and a sync with sales on any active co-sell deals. Fifteen minutes is usually enough. This is the meeting that catches a stalled deal before the protection window lapses.

**Monthly.** A partner activity review across all tiers: who's gone quiet and needs outreach or an exit conversation, and how new partner onboarding is progressing. This is where the health scoring data (see `partner-health-scoring-and-dashboards.md`) starts to matter even between formal quarterly scoring cycles.

**Quarterly, top tier only.** A real QBR, not a status update. Agenda: revenue review (sourced and influenced, against plan), pipeline review (active, stuck, and newly registered deals), direct feedback from the partner on what's working and what isn't, a refreshed account mapping session if the client's motion uses one, a plan for the next quarter (target accounts, co-marketing, enablement gaps), and executive alignment on strategic direction. A QBR that skips the direct feedback step tends to drift into a one-way status report, which defeats the purpose of having the partner in the room.

**Annually.** A full program review: tier reassignment based on actual performance rather than tenure, a look at whether the commission structure still makes sense, an honest ROI read on the program as a whole, and strategic planning for the next year's partner mix.

## Maturity gates, not a calendar

Most partner program roadmaps describe maturity by month (build the basics in the first six months, scale in the next twelve). That's a reasonable rough guide, but it puts the calendar in charge of a decision that should be made by evidence. The better model borrowed from stage-gate project management: a program moves to the next phase because it met the exit criteria for the current one, not because a certain number of months passed. A program that hasn't hit its Phase 1 criteria by month nine should stay in Phase 1, and a program that hits them by month three shouldn't be held back from Phase 2 just because a plan said six months.

### Phase 1: Foundation

**Exit criteria, all required:**
- An Ideal Partner Profile exists and has actually been used to screen candidates, not just written and shelved.
- The deal registration schema (see `ideal-partner-profile-and-crm-schema.md`) is live and being used, even if it's a spreadsheet rather than a CRM object.
- At least three partners are signed, onboarded, and have registered at least one deal each.
- Someone (even part-time, even the founder) is named as the owner of this program specifically.

**What not to build yet:** a tiering structure with more than two tiers, a portal, or a health scoring dashboard. There isn't enough partner activity yet to make any of these meaningful, and building them early usually means guessing at thresholds that will need to be thrown out once real data exists.

### Phase 2: Structure

**Exit criteria, all required:**
- A tiering structure is in place and at least one partner has moved tiers based on actual performance, proving the criteria are being applied and not just documented.
- A portal or equivalent self-serve resource is live, with usage being tracked (not just built and assumed to be used).
- Health scoring is running for at least the top tier, on the cadence defined above.
- Partner-sourced and partner-influenced revenue can be reported separately, with confidence, not as an estimate.

**What not to build yet:** account mapping technology, a dedicated partner team beyond the current owner, or marketing development funds. These are Phase 3 investments and tend to get justified prematurely by enthusiasm rather than by the data this phase is supposed to produce.

### Phase 3: Scale

**Exit criteria, all required:**
- Partner-sourced and partner-influenced revenue combined make up a meaningful, sustained share of total revenue (the actual target number depends entirely on the client's motion and deal size, and should be set from their own trailing data, not imported from a benchmark).
- The program has survived at least one full annual review cycle, including at least one tier reassignment and one partner exit, proving the governance actually functions rather than just existing on paper.
- A dedicated partner function (a real hire, not "and also partner stuff" on someone's plate) is in place or funded.

**What this phase actually needs:** this is genuinely where account mapping tools, co-marketing budget, and a larger partner team start to earn their cost, because Phase 1 and 2 will have already proven the underlying mechanics work at small scale. Scaling a mechanic that doesn't work at five partners just produces the same failure at fifty, faster and more expensively.

## Applying this to a Blueprint audit

When auditing an existing program (see the audit framework in `partner-ecosystem-builder`), use these gates as a diagnostic: which phase is the program actually in, based on evidence, regardless of which phase its size or age would suggest. A program with forty partners and no working attribution schema is still in Phase 1, no matter how long it's been running, and the fix has to start there rather than at whatever phase the partner count implies.
